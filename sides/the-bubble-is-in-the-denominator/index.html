Who is on each side · Part 10

Is the S&P 500 cheap at 19 times earnings, or is the denominator borrowed?

12 investors who disagree about The Bubble Is in the Denominator. Each voice is attributed and paraphrased, dated where the date is known, with a link where one exists.

The DeepStack callSkeptical

The market is not cheap. It is capitalizing AI earnings before anyone knows who owns them.

Cheap is conditional · Conviction High (4/5)

Read the full call

The bull case · 5

Profits carried the rally

  1. RenOct 5, 2026

    Called it the cheapest bubble ever seen: semiconductors at 17.1 times forward earnings against 19.0 for the S&P 500, with profits, not multiples, carrying the rally; the largest spenders have cash and long-term contracts.

  2. Michael Howell

    Underestimated AI spending’s lift to earnings; higher yields show an economy running hotter than policy, not solvency fear; past cycles began to buckle near 5.5%, so the next move may be down.

  3. David George

    Credits technology with about 76% of S&P 500 profit growth through late August; rental rates and residual values were supposed to fall and have not, with A100 rental prices no lower than in January.

    Source

  4. Tae Kim

    Relays Jensen Huang’s case that Nvidia’s GPUs are productive, fungible and durable, and puts OpenAI’s annualized revenue near $70 billion.

  5. Kris

    AWS has had more demand for AI capacity than it could supply despite large Nvidia orders and Amazon’s own chips; the cloud is capacity-constrained.

The bear case · 7

The buildout is paying itself

  1. Michael BurryOct 1, 2026

    Nvidia’s residual-value slide is a model output, not a second-hand price, and not even internally consistent; GPU server prices should fall sharply when the memory shortage turns into a glut. Bought more far out-of-the-money MetLife puts expiring in 2029.

  2. Torsten Slok

    Technology’s forecasts roughly double the sector’s operating cash flow by 2028 while its customers’ forecasts grow far more slowly; total factor productivity is still a little under zero. Who exactly will be writing all those checks.

    Source

  3. Mia Silverio

    Half of this year’s earnings growth comes from AI investment, on a Goldman Sachs estimate; the bubble is in the labs rather than the market as a whole, and a 5.3% riskless Treasury makes stock buyers pay less for each dollar of profit.

    Source

  4. Scott Galloway

    Ties AI fragility to the absence of regulation; the Magnificent Seven make up a third of the index while the buildout drove a 32% increase in S&P earnings growth this year.

    Source

  5. Kakashii

    Nvidia’s plan to ship more than 10 million Blackwell and Rubin chips by the end of 2026 implies roughly 20 gigawatts of power; Blackwell GPUs ended up in warehouses because too few data centers had cooling and equipment.

  6. Michael Spencer

    Ramp’s index showed what customers pay per million tokens down 41% from its March peak, to $0.68; cheaper intelligence can compress the revenue needed to repay the hardware and the bonds.

  7. TSCS

    Insurers that once absorbed long bonds now have arithmetic reasons to stay away; from the February 27 low the 10-year rose 104 basis points, 96 of them real, while the decade-ahead inflation forecast barely moved.

The bull case · 1

An economy running hot, not a solvency scare

  1. Michael Howell

    Higher yields show an economy running hotter than policy is set for; with nominal yields above 5% and real yields near 3%, this is the best entry point in years and the next move may be down.

The bear case · 4

The riskless bond reprices every dollar of profit

  1. Mia Silverio

    With 5.3% available on a Treasury carrying no credit risk, stock buyers demand a higher return and pay less for each dollar of profit.

    Source

  2. TSCS

    Insurers that once absorbed long bonds now have arithmetic reasons to stay away; from the February 27 low the 10-year rose 104 basis points, 96 of them real.

  3. Steve Eisman

    Calls 5% the Rubicon, while noting that oil and the 10-year are the two variables that matter and neither is predictable.

  4. Vitaliy Katsenelson

    Would not personally buy a 10-year Treasury even at 6%.

Notes

Not a disagreement.

Where two voices only seem to differ, or where the thread runs back to an earlier Part.

  • Bridge to Part 08

    Slok’s question, who writes the checks, is the same test Part 08 set for hyperscaler cash flow reaching $2 trillion by 2030; the late-October earnings resolve both.

  • Not a disagreement

    Ren and Burry both expect server prices to fall; they differ on whether the earnings built on today’s scarcity survive that fall.

Where they cross

Who answers whom.

The specific points where one voice meets another: the same evidence read two ways, or the same mechanism with a different sign.

  • Michael Burry contradicts David George on evidence

    George says A100s rent at January prices; Burry says the residual values on Nvidia’s slide are outputs of an eight-year rental model and expects server prices to fall when memory scarcity becomes a glut.

  • Torsten Slok contradicts Ren on evidence

    Ren says profits carried the rally and the largest spenders have cash and contracts; Slok says technology’s forecasts and its customers’ forecasts cannot both be right, so someone else has to write the checks.

  • Michael Howell contradicts TSCS on mechanism

    Howell reads yields above 5% as an economy running hot and the best entry point in years; TSCS sees a long end losing its hedged foreign buyers and still rising.

  • Ren contradicts Michael Spencer on evidence

    Spencer’s Ramp index shows the price per million tokens down 41% from its March peak; Ren counters that OpenAI’s newest model charges more per token than its predecessor.

  • Ren crosses sides with Kakashii on timing

    Ren, on the bull side, still warns that chip designs turn over faster than factories can be rebuilt; Kakashii holds that Blackwell GPUs ended up in warehouses for lack of cooling and equipment.

  • Mia Silverio agrees with Scott Galloway on scope

    Both tie the earnings surge to AI investment; Silverio narrows the bubble to the labs, Galloway widens it to the third of the index that the Magnificent Seven make up.
